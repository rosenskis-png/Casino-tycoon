CASINO TYCOON: NORTH STAR
A timeless statement of what this game is and what it is trying to be.

==================================================================
WHAT THIS GAME IS
==================================================================

Casino Tycoon is a management game in the spirit of RollerCoaster Tycoon. The player builds and runs a casino: the floor, the rooms, the grounds around it, and the attractions that feed it. Their job is to shape a place that real-feeling people want to spend time in, and to keep it profitable. Safety, decency, and legality are choices, not requirements.

The game is sometimes serious and deep, sometimes satirical and lighthearted. It aims for the nostalgia and freedom of the original RollerCoaster Tycoon: a toybox where you can build something beautiful, run it brilliantly, or abuse it gleefully, and the simulation responds honestly to all three.

The player manages the casino and its grounds only. Hotel rooms, parking, and the wider resort are outside the game. Where they matter, they appear as things the player can see and act on from the floor, such as guests arriving from the hotel elevator, or comps.

The game plays on a phone. It should feel native to touch, readable at a glance, and satisfying in both short and long sessions.

==================================================================
THE CENTRAL PROBLEM
==================================================================

In aggregate, the house always wins. A casino game that ignores this becomes a spreadsheet where money piles up on its own. Every system in this game exists to put skill somewhere other than "build machines and wait."

Skill lives in five places:

1. Reading the market. Every scenario has a population of potential guests, and each kind wants something different. The player works out who is coming, who could be coming, and whom to build for.

2. Shaping the floor. Layout, rooms, theming, and placement change how people feel, how long they stay, how fast they play, where they walk, and whether they come back. This is the heart of the game.

3. Plugging leaks. Cheats, dishonest staff, drunks, broken machines, and rarer larger threats all drain the house edge. Telling a cheat from a lucky honest guest, and deciding what to do about it, is a judgment with real consequences either way.

4. Surviving variance. Profit is certain only over the long run. Jackpots, whales, and hot streaks can sink a casino in a bad stretch. Insurance, table limits, cash on hand, and credit are how the player gets through.

5. Running the calendar. The casino never closes. Demand, crowds, and staffing needs change with the season, the event calendar, and the casino's own marketing. A floor that is right for convention week can be wrong for the slow months.

==================================================================
THE CREATIVE CORE
==================================================================

RollerCoaster Tycoon gave players something to build and be proud of, and then measured it. Casino Tycoon does the same in two ways.

Designing games. The player designs their own slot machines: how much they pay back, how volatile they are, how often they pay anything at all, how losses are dressed up with lights and sound, how they look, and what they are themed around. Table game rules can be tuned the same way. Every creation is judged by the market. Different guests want different machines, and a machine built to trap one crowd may repel another.

Shaping the space. The player builds and tears down walls to make rooms, develops the grounds outside, buys neighboring land where a scenario allows it, and dresses everything with decoration and theming. A room with a clear identity, done with taste, should feel better to guests than the sum of its parts.

The game never tells the player whether a creation is good. Guests show them.

==================================================================
THE HIDDEN PSYCHOLOGY LAYER
==================================================================

Beneath the visible casino is a hidden model of how space affects people. Every tile carries qualities such as foot traffic, noise and energy, prestige, privacy, how visible the exits are, surveillance, crowding, cleanliness, and theming. Every object and choice changes these qualities around it, and every kind of guest likes and dislikes them in different measure.

Qualities spread outward and fade with distance rather than switching on and off at a boundary. Many sources in one place add up, though guests' response to any one quality has limits. Walls and other solid barriers weaken what passes through them without blocking it entirely, so a neighboring room is felt, but less.

Theming has a hidden layer of its own. Decorations belong to visible theme categories or to a general category, but every piece also carries hidden affinities: places it suits, places it doesn't, and themes it complements or clashes with. Some themes combine into something better than either alone, and some undermine each other. A general decoration can quietly strengthen a theme it suits, and mixing styles well is rewarded. None of this is ever listed for the player.

Four rules govern this layer:

Hidden weights, visible symptoms. The player never sees the formulas. They see the results: what guests think and say, where they walk, how long they sit, how fast they play, what they spend, and whether they return. Symptoms can be general. One guest who dislikes an area's theming is noise; a room full of them is a diagnosis. The player starts with symptoms alone, and deeper views, such as maps of the hidden qualities or tools for reading individual guests, are earned. Information is itself a resource.

Grounded, not invented. The psychology is modeled on how real casinos are designed and on what research supports. Every mechanic must be measurable and implementable in the simulation. Casino folklore without evidence is left out, or appears only as a trap that costs money and does nothing.

No correct answers. Every choice has pros and cons and suits some guests while repelling others. A maze-like floor or a grand open one, a loud hub or a quiet refuge, a visible security presence, easy cash access, strict or lenient house rules: each is right for someone and wrong for someone else. The game never labels a strategy good or bad. The market decides.

Organized by lever, not by trick. A single lever, such as where the slot machines go, serves many goals at once. The design thinks in terms of everything a lever can do and what it costs, not in lists of tricks aimed at single outcomes.

==================================================================
GUESTS
==================================================================

Guests are the center of the simulation and its main source of feedback. Each guest is an individual with:

- A type, drawn from recognizable casino archetypes, each with its own tastes, budget, seasons, games, drinking habits, and tolerances.
- Company. Many guests arrive with others: couples, families, groups of friends, parties. Groups move and decide together, and what happens to one member affects the rest.
- Intentions. Not everyone comes to gamble. Some come to eat, drink, see a show, dance, or pass through on their way somewhere else, and some of them end up gambling anyway.
- A way of playing. How much they bet, how fast they play, whether they walk away when ahead or chase until broke, whether they nurse a cheap machine for free drinks. Two guests at the same machine can be worth very different amounts to the house.
- Inhibition. Drinks and other substances change how guests bet, spend, withdraw cash, and behave. Some stay sober, some have a drink or two, and some go much further, on purpose or not.
- Physical needs that pull them toward amenities or out the door.
- A mood shaped by the space around them, their luck, their company, and how they are treated.
- Hidden traits. A few guests are luckier or unluckier than the math suggests. A small number are cheats. None of this is visible, and a cheat looks exactly like anyone else.
- A voice. Guests think out loud, and their thoughts are the player's main diagnostic tool.

Guests react to each other's behavior, not to who they are. A family is not driven away by young partiers existing, but by the noise, the fights, and the mess that can come with them. Most guests enjoy a little drama. Some want none, and some want a lot.

What guests experience becomes reputation, tracked separately for each kind of guest. A casino can be beloved by one crowd and avoided by another. Reputation moves slowly, so changing who a casino is for is a real strategic commitment with a dangerous stretch in the middle.

Guests value time more than money. A good visit is measured by how long a guest's money lasted and how good it felt, not by whether they won. A floor that empties wallets too fast wins today and loses tomorrow.

High rollers and whales are events in themselves, with preferences and requests. Meeting those requests is always optional. A whale who is not accommodated simply leaves sooner, taking their potential losses, and potential winnings, with them.

==================================================================
INCIDENTS AND HOUSE RULES
==================================================================

Most of the time a casino is a room full of quiet people gambling. Around them, life happens: celebration, drunkenness, flirting, arguments, fights, spills, mess, people passing out, winners buying rounds, losers breaking down, underage guests trying their luck, escorts working the floor, couples finding a quiet corner. How often these things happen depends on who the casino attracts and what the house tolerates.

The player sets how strictly their own security enforces the rules, category by category. Too strict drives away the crowds who came for a good time. Too lenient drives away the crowds who didn't, and eventually draws outside attention. Guests who dislike what they see report it, and reports that go unanswered escalate to the police.

==================================================================
LEAKAGE, CHEATS, AND ENFORCEMENT
==================================================================

Cheats are a regular part of running a casino, not a rare catastrophe. Over a scenario the player faces a meaningful number of them. Each one matters, and none should decide a scenario alone. Cheats play honestly part of the time to avoid notice, and when they cheat, they are after serious money.

The player rarely knows for certain who is cheating. They suspect, based on who is winning, how long they play, and what their earned tools reveal, and lucky honest guests keep that suspicion genuinely uncertain. Research unlocks better ways to read guests, but short of catching someone in the act, no tool is ever certain.

Any guest can be marked as suspicious. Marked guests draw more attention from security and cameras, making a catch more likely, and the player can choose to be alerted when they leave or return.

The player can hire enforcers and act on any guest: a warning, a lifetime ban, a beating, or making them disappear. These options exist for cheats but work on anyone. Used rarely, on a lone guest who really was cheating, the consequences are small. Used often, on an innocent guest, on someone whose company will notice they are gone, or on someone important, the consequences grow severe.

Leakage also comes from inside: dishonest staff, skimming, and theft from the cage.

==================================================================
DARK LEVERS
==================================================================

Like the original RollerCoaster Tycoon, the game lets the player be cruel, on purpose or by accident. Blocking the exits, charging for restrooms, routing the only way out past an ATM and a paid show, pushing drinks to keep people betting, rigging machines, tolerating vice for profit, using violence: these levers work, sometimes very well, and they carry heavy costs inside the simulation in the form of angry guests, collapsing reputation, incidents, and trouble with the police and the regulator.

These penalties are heavy but recoverable. A player who experiments with cruelty and then fixes it can climb back, slowly.

==================================================================
WHAT THE PLAYER BUILDS AND MANAGES
==================================================================

Games. Slot machines, table games, and other forms of gambling in many varieties, including machines of the player's own design. Variety must be meaningful: each game differs in house edge, volatility, pace, footprint, staffing, who it appeals to, and how it changes the space around it. Two items that differ only in a number are one item.

Amenities as places. Bars, restaurants, restrooms, shows, clubs, and similar spaces, indoors and out, are areas with real room in them, not boxes people vanish into. Guests visibly enter, sit, eat, drink, and watch. Size matters: bigger spaces seat more and can become finer versions of themselves.

The building and its grounds. Walls, rooms, outdoor areas, and land that can be bought where a scenario allows it. How much room there is to grow varies by scenario, and space itself is a constraint.

Decoration and theming. Themes are available from the start or unlocked through research. Decoration shapes the qualities of the space around it and the identity of each room.

Staff. Workers the player hires and positions, each with a job that matters and each visible on the floor doing it: cleaners, technicians, security, enforcers, drink servers, supervisors, and more. Some come attached to what they serve, such as dealers at tables. Staff are people too: they can be skilled or sloppy, content or resentful, honest or dishonest.

Policies. Levers that are set rather than placed: limits, prices, drink strength and service, comps, insurance, marketing, credit, and how strictly house rules are enforced. They keep the game active between construction projects.

Research. New games, tools, themes, and insight unlock over time within each scenario. Research expands both what the player can build and what they can see.

Placement is physical. Things face a direction, need access, take up room, and affect their neighbors. Aisles, seating, sightlines, walls, and adjacency are all part of the puzzle.

==================================================================
MONEY, RISK, AND THE LAW
==================================================================

The house edge is real and honest. Every game is mathematically consistent, and the casino earns what the math says it should over time.

Variance is real too. Jackpots, hot tables, lucky guests, and big players can swing a stretch of time. Ordinary loans are available against the casino's worth, at meaningful interest. A casino that cannot pay what it owes can borrow in an emergency, but only at a steep price, and needing to is a public scandal. A casino that cannot pay even then is in serious trouble. Risk tools cost money on average, but they buy survival.

Leakage is the enemy of the edge. Some of it stays invisible until the player learns to look for it.

Money comes from gaming first and everything else second. Amenities matter mostly for what they do to guests and traffic, not for what they earn on their own.

The casino answers to two outside authorities, and they care about different things. The police care about disorder, violence, drugs, vice, and the incidents guests report. The gaming regulator cares about the games themselves: rigged machines, skimmed revenue, unpaid winnings. Standing with each rises and falls separately, and either can close the casino. In some scenarios, some officials can be bribed.

==================================================================
TIME
==================================================================

The casino runs without end, across seasons and years. As in RollerCoaster Tycoon there is no time of day: the calendar counts days, months, and years, while guests live at floor pace and may happily gamble for what the calendar calls days or weeks. Seasons and events such as shows, fights, conventions, and holidays send crowds onto the floor on a schedule. The player plans for rhythms, peaks, and slow stretches, not a static state.

As in RollerCoaster Tycoon, the calendar moves quickly while the floor moves at a human pace. What the player watches is what the simulation is doing: each spin of the reels is a real round of wagers, and it shows a jackpot only if one truly hit. Money is game money, tuned for play the way RollerCoaster Tycoon's was, while the prices guests see look real. The numbers are always exact. The player can pause or run the game at several speeds, and at the fastest speeds the animation may become more approximate, but the underlying math never changes.

==================================================================
PROGRESSION AND SCENARIOS
==================================================================

The game is a sequence of scenarios in the RollerCoaster Tycoon tradition. A few are open at the start, and completing one unlocks the next. Scenarios vary widely in size, length, and budget, from a small local operation to a sprawling empire.

- Early scenarios are small and approachable, with a limited toolset and plenty of freedom in how to win. They teach the player to read guests and symptoms without spelling out the rules.
- Core scenarios are each built around a small set of psychological ideas. The setting, the guest mix, and the goal are arranged so the player must discover and use those ideas to win, without being told what they are. Each one has an intuitive wrong move that visibly fails.
- Challenge scenarios bend the rules with a gimmick, an unusual goal, an inverted incentive, or an outlandish scale.
- Free play offers an open lot and adjustable conditions.

Most scenarios have both a money goal and a reputation goal, so neither can be chased at the total expense of the other. Either may be the hard one. Money goals may be measured in cash or in the casino's worth. Reputation goals may be measured at a moment or held over a stretch of time, and often concern a particular kind of guest. Some challenge scenarios replace these with something else entirely.

==================================================================
LOOK, FEEL, AND TONE
==================================================================

The game is pixel art in the spirit of classic tycoon games, set on busy casino carpet with brass, felt, and neon. The floor is alive. People walk, sit, drink, dance, argue, and react. Reels spin and land. Cards are dealt. Wheels turn. Doors open. Every mechanic is visible on the floor, not only in a menu. Watching the casino run is part of the reward, and the player can zoom in close on a single table or out far enough to take in the whole property.

The casino sounds like a casino. The noise of the floor, the machines, the tables, and the crowd is part of how the player reads the space.

The tone is warm and gently satirical, like the original: a toybox view of a very real industry. This is a game about sin city, and it is allowed to be dark. It shows what casinos do to people, and what the player chooses to do, plainly, without lecturing and without looking away. What keeps it a toybox is scale and style: small, quick, low-resolution animation, with no gore, no nudity, and no lingering on suffering.

The interface feels like a casino tycoon's control room: clear, tactile, and quick on a phone, with the game world always the star. News reaches the player through a single, calm, always-present ticker, with a full log for anything they missed. Color signals importance, and only what truly matters is marked urgent.

==================================================================
BUILD PRINCIPLES
==================================================================

- Data drives the game. Games, objects, themes, guest types, behaviors, incidents, staff, research, scenarios, sounds, and art are defined as data. Adding a new thing means adding an entry, not rewriting systems.
- Mechanics are modules. Each system plugs into the simulation clock independently, and changing or removing one does not break the others.
- The interface never changes the game directly. It issues commands, and the simulation carries them out.
- Simulation first, spectacle second. Visuals summarize real simulated state and never misrepresent it.
- The game stays smooth on a phone at every scale it offers. The simulation is built to grow without lagging.
- Released saves carry forward into new versions.
- Every release ships as readable source and a single self-contained playable file.

==================================================================
WHAT THIS GAME IS NOT
==================================================================

- Not real gambling. No real money is ever involved. The player can sit down at their own games and gamble the house's fictional money as a diversion, but running the casino is the game.
- Not a hotel or resort manager.
- Not a spreadsheet. Numbers exist to serve decisions the player can see and feel on the floor.
- Not a morality play, and not an endorsement.
- Not built on real brands, real casinos, or licensed characters. Everything is original.

==================================================================
THE LITMUS TEST
==================================================================

Before anything is added, changed, or kept, it should pass these questions:

- Does it feed one of the five places where skill lives, or give the player something to create and be proud of?
- Is it a real tradeoff that suits some guests or situations and not others?
- Is it grounded in how casinos and people actually work, and can the simulation measure it?
- Will the player see its effects on the floor or hear about them from guests?
- Does it make the floor more alive to watch?
- Can it be added as data or as a module without disturbing everything else?
